First off, welcome to the Bulgarian property hunt! The biggest hurdle you need to know about right away is the land ownership rule. As a non-EU citizen, you cannot own land directly in your own name due to constitutional restrictions. However, you *can* own the building itself. To get around the land restriction, most non-EU buyers set up a Bulgarian limited liability company (EOOD or OOD). The company then purchases both the land and the property legally.

Here is the step-by-step process I went through last year: 1) Find the property and negotiate the price. 2) Hire an independent local lawyer (do not use the agent's recommended one!). 3) Sign a preliminary contract and pay a 10% deposit. 4) If you are a non-EU citizen, register your Bulgarian company during this time. 5) Conduct background checks for debts and mortgages. 6) Sign the final Title Deed (Notary Act) in front of a Bulgarian notary. 7) Register the property with the Bulgargaz, EVN (electricity), and local municipality.

Watch out for hidden costs! They add up really fast. Beyond the purchase price, you need to budget for notary fees (usually 0.5% to 1% depending on the property value), local municipality tax (around 2-3%), agency commission (usually 3%), and legal fees. If you set up a company, factor in accounting fees and annual tax filings too. Also, check if there are any unpaid maintenance fees from the previous owner, especially if it's in a gated coastal complex.

A massive pitfall to avoid is buying 'on green' (off-plan developments) from shady developers. Many foreigners got burned during the 2008 crash and even recently, where developers took deposits and never finished the building, or worse, mortgaged the land without telling the buyers. Always buy completed properties with Act 16 (certificate of occupancy) if possible.

Regarding the company route for non-EU citizens: maintaining a Bulgarian company has ongoing costs. You'll need a registered address, a local accountant (costs around 50-100 euros a month), and you have to file annual financial statements even if the company has zero activity. Make sure these recurring costs fit into your holiday home budget.

Make sure your lawyer does a thorough check at the Bulgarian Property Register to confirm the seller is actually the legal owner and that there are no third-party claims, encumbrances, or bank seizures on the apartment. People have lost money because they handed over a deposit to someone who didn't have the full rights to sell.

If you are looking at coastal complexes (like Sunny Beach or Sveti Vlas), pay very close attention to the annual maintenance fees. Some management companies charge exorbitant yearly fees (sometimes 10-15 euros per square meter) for pool and garden upkeep, and they can sue you or restrict access if you don't pay. Always review the maintenance contract *before* signing the preliminary agreement.

Tip for transferring money: Don't use traditional high-street banks for the final payment if you can avoid it, as their exchange rates and transfer fees for BGN/EUR will eat a huge chunk of your money. Use a regulated currency transfer service like Wise or Revolut to save thousands on the conversion, but make sure your Bulgarian notary is okay with how the funds arrive.

One thing many foreigners forget is that owning property in Bulgaria does *not* automatically grant you permanent residency. While there is a 'Property Owner' visa category, it has strict financial requirements and doesn't give you full EU freedom of movement. If your goal is to live there permanently, talk to an immigration lawyer alongside your property lawyer.

Always get a Bulgarian tax number (BULSTAT) as soon as you start the process. Your lawyer can help you with this. You'll need it for practically everything, from opening a local bank account to registering the property and paying your annual local taxes.
